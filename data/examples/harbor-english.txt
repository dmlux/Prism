THE LIGHT AT CEDAR POINT

CHAPTER 1

When Dr. Eleanor Hart arrived at Cedar Point on the 3rd of
November, 1951, she carried two suitcases, a crate of books, and
a brass kettle polished until it shone. The mail boat drew up to
the little pier in the grey light of dawn, and the keeper met her
without many words. His name was Mr. Samuel Brooks, he was
fifty-two years old, and he had lived alone on the point for
eleven years.

"You'll take the north room," he said, and lifted the heaviest
suitcase onto his shoulder. "It's cold, but it's dry. Supper is
at half past six, e.g. porridge on the days the boat is late."

Eleanor had come to catalogue the birds. The university had
funded the survey — approx. two hundred species were said to
nest along the cliffs — and she meant to record every one. She
had read the notes of the previous naturalist, a Mr. T. Alden,
whose handwriting ran uphill and whose counts, she suspected,
were generous.

The lighthouse itself was older than the records. Built in 1841,
rebuilt after the storm of 1889, it stood ninety feet above the
water, and on clear nights its beam reached the shipping lanes
eight miles out. Below it the sea was the colour of slate. Gulls
turned in the wind above the boat-\nhouse and the drying nets.

"Is this real work," she asked, "or a way to keep a lamp lit?"
Mr. Brooks did not answer at once. He set the suitcase down at
the foot of the stairs and looked at her.

"Both, Dr. Hart. The Trinity House pays for the light. The birds
are mine." He almost smiled. "Cf. the logbook, if you doubt me;
I have counted the terns since '43."

She unpacked slowly. The room faced the open water.Every surface
carried a fine salt haze that returned within a day of wiping,
and the window rattled in its frame when the wind came from the
north-east. Still, it was hers, and by evening she had pinned a
tide table to the wall and set her instruments in a row: a
barometer, a field glass, two notebooks, and a battered watch
that lost four minutes a day.

The first entry in her own log was dated Nov. 3 and read, in a
hand far steadier than Mr. Alden's: "Arrived Cedar Point, 6.40
a.m. Wind NE, force 4. Kittiwakes on the south face; one
guillemot, possibly two. The keeper says the fog comes up from
the harbour without warning — an upper bound, not a promise."

They fell into a routine. Before the light was extinguished at
dawn Mr. Brooks would climb the tower, and Eleanor, wrapped
against the cold, would already be on the cliff path with her
glass. She learned to tell the birds apart in poor light: the
sharp call of a tern, the heavier wingbeat of a fulmar, the way
a shag holds its neck. She wrote to a colleague at the museum,
E. Whitaker, at ewhitaker@museum.example.org, and to the survey
office, whose forms she found online at
https://www.example.org/coastal-survey and filled out by lamp.

By December the counts had grown. Number and gender were easy
enough for the ducks; the gulls resisted her, as gulls resist
everyone. She noted, in the margin, that Mr. Alden had missed the
Manx shearwaters entirely, e.g. the colony on the western
skerry, where at dusk the birds came in low over the water and
vanished into their burrows without a sound.

"You lead him by a mile on the shearwaters," she told the keeper
one evening, "and by rather less on the terns."

He weighed this. "The terns are mine," he said again, but there
was no edge in it. Outside, the light swung its slow arc across
the harbour, and the sea, invisible now, went on breathing
against the rocks. Somewhere below, in the dark, the shearwaters
were still coming in.
